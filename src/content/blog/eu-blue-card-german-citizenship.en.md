**With an EU Blue Card you can apply for German citizenship once you have lived in Germany lawfully for five years.** You do not need to switch to permanent residence first: the Blue Card is explicitly listed as a qualifying permit in [§ 10 StAG](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/stag/__10.html). You still need B1 German, a passed naturalization test, secure income and the other general requirements.

This is study information, not legal advice. Your local naturalization authority decides on your case.

## Typical timeline for Blue Card holders

| Milestone | When | Legal basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EU Blue Card issued | Year 0 | Residence Act |
| Settlement permit (permanent residence) possible | After 27 months of Blue Card employment, or **21 months with B1 German** | [§ 18c (2) AufenthG](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/aufenthg_2004/__18c.html) |
| German citizenship possible | After **5 years** of lawful residence | § 10 StAG |

Permanent residence is optional on the way to citizenship, but many Blue Card holders take it because it no longer depends on a specific job.

## Is there still a 3-year Blue Card route?

**No.** From June 2024 the law allowed naturalization after three years for people with "special integration achievements" (for example C1 German and outstanding professional performance). This route was abolished in October 2025; paragraph 3 of § 10 StAG is now marked as repealed. Many relocation blogs still mention three years, so check the publication date of anything you read.

Time spent in Germany before your Blue Card can count towards the five years if your stay was lawful, for example as a student or with another residence permit. Ask your authority how it calculates your residence periods.

## Requirements for Blue Card holders

Being a Blue Card holder does not replace the general requirements:

- **5 years** of lawful, habitual residence
- a valid EU Blue Card or settlement permit at the time of naturalization
- **German at B1** level, proven with a certificate
- the **naturalization test** passed: at least 17 of 33 questions correct
- you can support yourself and your family without Bürgergeld
- no criminal convictions
- commitment to the Basic Law and acknowledgement of Germany's historical responsibility

The full list with documents and fees is in our guide to [German citizenship requirements](/en/blog/german-citizenship-requirements/).

## Do I have to give up my current passport?

Germany has allowed multiple citizenship since 27 June 2024, so German law does not require you to renounce it. Your country of origin may still have its own rules. India, for example, does not allow dual citizenship, so Indian Blue Card holders should check the consequences with Indian authorities before applying.

## What about my family?

Your spouse or registered partner and your minor children can be naturalized together with you, even if they have not yet lived in Germany for five years themselves. Adult family members still need to meet the other requirements that apply to them, such as language and the test.

## The naturalization test for busy professionals

The test is in German, but it covers civics, history and everyday life rather than grammar. Short daily sessions work well:

1. Read each question with its [English translation](/en/fragen/) until you know the topic.
2. Switch to the German wording, which is what you will see in the exam.
3. Learn the [10 questions for your federal state](/en/fragen/#bundeslaender).
4. Take [33-question practice exams](/en/exam/) until you pass comfortably above 17.
5. [Book a test appointment](/en/blog/anmeldung-pruefung-schritte/) early, because centres in large cities fill up.

The [Leben in Deutschland app](/en/app/) keeps your progress and mistakes on your phone, so you can practise on the commute.
