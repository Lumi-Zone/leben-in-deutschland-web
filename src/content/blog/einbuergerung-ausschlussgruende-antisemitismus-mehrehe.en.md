You have the residence period, German language skills, the naturalisation test and a secure livelihood, and you are still not naturalised? This can happen if there is a **ground for exclusion** (Ausschlussgrund). The most important ones are set out in [Section 11 of the Nationality Act](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/stag/__11.html): **extremist activities**, an **untrue commitment**, **serious security reasons**, **polygamy** and conduct that disregards **equality between men and women**. There is also a special rule for criminal offences: **acts motivated by antisemitism, racism or other contempt for human beings** count even if the sentence was very small.

Since the reform in June 2024, these rules have been worded more strictly and more clearly. Here you will find out what each ground for exclusion means and how offices check it.

> **Note:** This article provides general information, not legal advice. Whether a ground for exclusion applies always depends on the individual case. If you are unsure, get advice before you apply.

## Key points

| Ground for exclusion | Legal basis | In brief |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Extremist activities | Section 11 sentence 1 no. 1 StAG | Factual indications that you pursue or support activities against the basic order or security; exception if you credibly show you have turned away. |
| Untrue commitment | Section 11 sentence 1 no. 1a StAG | Factual indications that your commitment does not reflect your conviction ("lip service"). |
| Particularly serious interest in expulsion | Section 11 sentence 1 no. 2 StAG | Particularly serious security reasons under Section 54(1) no. 2 or 4 of the Residence Act. |
| Polygamy | Section 11 sentence 1 no. 3 a StAG | You are married to more than one person at the same time. |
| Disregard for equality | Section 11 sentence 1 no. 3 b StAG | Your conduct shows that you disregard equality between men and women. |
| Offence motivated by contempt for human beings | Section 12a(1) sentence 2 StAG | Even small sentences count if the judgment finds an antisemitic, racist or other motive of contempt for human beings. |

## 1. Extremist activities

Naturalisation is excluded if **factual indications** (tatsächliche Anhaltspunkte) justify the assumption that you pursue or support, or have pursued or supported in the past, activities that

- are directed against the free democratic basic order or against the existence or security of the Federation or a federal state,
- aim to unlawfully impair the conduct of office of constitutional bodies, or
- endanger Germany's foreign interests through violence or preparations for violence.

A conviction is not necessary for this. Reliable facts are enough, for example findings by the Office for the Protection of the Constitution (Verfassungsschutz) about membership or active support. Many offices routinely make enquiries with the Office for the Protection of the Constitution and the police; this is shown, for example, by the overview of Lower Saxony offices in **[Naturalisation in Lower Saxony](/en/blog/einbuergerung-niedersachsen-bekenntnis-befragung/)**.

**Exception:** If you credibly show that you have **turned away** from earlier activities, this does not stand in the way of naturalisation. A simple "I have nothing to do with that any more" is usually not enough. Verifiable evidence helps, for example having left an organisation, a long period without activities and a recognisable inner distancing. The **[declaration of loyalty](/en/blog/loyalitaetserklaerung-einbuergerung/)** has a separate option for this.

## 2. Untrue commitment

Since 2024, the law expressly states that the commitment to the free democratic basic order and to Germany's historical responsibility must be true in substance. If there are factual indications that it is not, naturalisation is excluded.

Such indications can arise, for example, from public statements, social media posts, findings by security authorities or answers given in the interview with the office. This is shown by the Regensburg case, in which an applicant denied Israel's right to exist: **[Israel's right to exist and German citizenship](/en/blog/einbuergerung-existenzrecht-israel/)**.

Conversely, offices may not simply assume doubts. In 2025, the Administrative Court of Braunschweig overturned a refusal that was based solely on questioning without a reason: **[Refused after 23 questions: the Peine ruling](/en/blog/einbuergerung-abgelehnt-23-fragen-peine-urteil/)**.

## 3. Serious security reasons

Naturalisation is also excluded if there is a **particularly serious interest in expulsion** under Section 54(1) no. 2 or no. 4 of the Residence Act (Aufenthaltsgesetz). This concerns serious threats to security, for example in connection with terrorism or with violence used to pursue political or religious aims. For the vast majority of applicants, this ground plays no role.

## 4. Polygamy

Anyone who is **married to more than one spouse at the same time** will not be naturalised. This also applies if the further marriage was validly concluded abroad under the law there.

**Background:** In 2018, the Federal Administrative Court ruled that polygamy does prevent naturalisation as the spouse of a German, but not automatically the general entitlement to naturalisation, because the principle of monogamy is not part of the free democratic basic order ([BVerwG, 1 C 15.17](https://www.bverwg.de/290518U1C15.17.0)). The legislature responded to this. Since the 2024 reform, polygamy has been an express ground for exclusion in Section 11 StAG.

Some offices ask directly about your attitude to polygamy in the interview, in Lower Saxony for example the City of Salzgitter.

## 5. Disregard for equality between men and women

Naturalisation is also excluded if you **show through your conduct** that you disregard the equality of men and women laid down in the Basic Law (Article 3(2) Basic Law: "Men and women shall have equal rights.").

The key word is **conduct**. It is not about private opinions alone, but about what someone does. This may apply, for example, if someone uses violence against their partner, forbids women in the family from working, getting an education or choosing their spouse, or treats daughters worse than sons because they are girls. Whether that is enough in an individual case is decided by the office and the courts based on the facts.

Offices often ask about this topic in the interview. The City of Wolfsburg, for example, stated that it refuses an application if the questioning reveals indications that someone does not recognise equality between men and women. How to answer such questions well is explained in the guide **[German citizenship interview questions](/en/blog/einbuergerung-gespraech-fragen-bekenntnis/)**.

## 6. Offences with an antisemitic or racist motive

As a rule, you must not have been convicted of an unlawful act to be naturalised. However, [Section 12a StAG](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/stag/__12a.html) makes exceptions for small sentences. The following are generally disregarded:

- educational measures or disciplinary measures under the Youth Courts Act (Jugendgerichtsgesetz),
- fines of up to 90 daily rates (Tagessätze),
- prison sentences of up to three months that were suspended on probation and remitted after the probation period.

**These exceptions do not apply** if you were convicted of an **antisemitic, racist or other act motivated by contempt for human beings** and the court established this motive in its judgment. In that case, even a small fine counts. This can concern, for example, insults, threats or damage to property directed against people because of their religion or origin.

An example from the naturalisation test: anyone who denies the Holocaust can be punished with imprisonment of up to five years or a fine ([question 96](/en/frage/96/)).

## What happens if a ground for exclusion only becomes known later?

If naturalisation was obtained through deception, threats, bribery or intentionally false or incomplete statements, it can be **withdrawn** under [Section 35 StAG](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/stag/__35.html), up to ten years after naturalisation. Anyone who conceals a further marriage or a membership, for example, still risks losing their German passport years later.

## What to do if you are unsure

1. **Be honest.** Concealing is almost always riskier than disclosing.
2. **Use the right option in the declaration of loyalty** if you previously had contacts with problematic groups.
3. **Collect evidence** that you have turned away or that your life situation has changed, for example a divorce certificate if you were previously in a polygamous marriage.
4. **Check your criminal record**, for example with a certificate of good conduct (Führungszeugnis), and, if anything is unclear, ask whether a motive was established in the judgment.
5. **Get advice** before you apply, for example from a migration advice service or a lawyer specialising in migration law.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a single social media post enough for a refusal?

That depends on the content and the context. What matters is whether factual indications justify the assumption that your commitment is untrue or that you support extremist activities. Clearly antisemitic, racist or violence-glorifying posts can be such an indication.

### I was married to two women but have since divorced one of them. Is that a problem?

The ground for exclusion concerns people who are married to more than one spouse **at the same time**. If only one marriage still exists, it no longer applies according to the wording of the law. Disclose the divorce with documents.

### Does the office automatically check with the Office for the Protection of the Constitution?

In many federal states, there are standard enquiries with security authorities. The answers of the Lower Saxony offices to the state parliament mention, for example, enquiries with the Office for the Protection of the Constitution, the police and the Federal Central Criminal Register (Bundeszentralregister).
