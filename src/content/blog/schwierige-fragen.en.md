Many people study a lot but still make mistakes on the same topics. Often it is not about effort. It is about similar answers, small words or terms that are easy to mix up.

Here are 10 difficult question types in the citizenship test and a simple study strategy.

## Three practice questions to start with

Answer the question first and then open the explanation. These examples are an editorial selection of typical mix-ups; they are not a ranking by error frequency.

| Topic | Practice | What to watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fundamental rights | [Question 19: What does freedom of movement mean?](/en/frage/19/) | Distinguish choosing where to live from choosing a job or a religion. |
| Constitutional bodies | [Question 85: Who makes up the Bundesrat?](/en/frage/85/) | Think about how the federal states are represented at federal level. |
| Voting rights | [Question 115: What does the active right to vote mean?](/en/frage/115/) | Separate "voting yourself" from "being able to be elected". |

If you have already practised on this website, you will find your previous mistakes under [revise your mistakes](/en/focus/). For a complete trial run, open the [exam simulation](/en/exam/).

## 1) Bundestag, Bundesrat, federal government
Their tasks are often confused.

- **Bundestag**: directly elected, passes laws.
- **Bundesrat**: represents the federal states, takes part in legislation.
- **Federal government**: carries out policy.

Tip: always learn these three institutions together, not separately.

## 2) Active and passive voting rights
These terms sound similar but mean different things.

- **Active**: voting yourself.
- **Passive**: being able to be elected.

Memory aid: "Active = action (I vote), passive = position (I am elected)".

## 3) Fundamental rights and their limits
Many questions check not only the right but also its legal limit.

Example: freedom of expression is central, but not unlimited. Criminal law limits still apply.

## 4) Federal, state and local level
Questions about the federal level, the states and municipalities are often difficult. All belong to the state, but they have different tasks.

Study tip: make a small table with three levels: federal, state, municipal. Write three typical tasks for each level.

## 5) Rule of law and separation of powers
With questions such as "Who controls whom?", mistakes happen quickly.

The basic idea matters: making laws, administering and judging are separate tasks.

## 6) History of the Federal Republic
Factual questions are hard if you study without an order.

Tip: work with a mini timeline and 6 to 8 key events.

## 7) The European Union
Terms such as the EU, the Council of Europe and Europe are often confused here.

Approach: write down a one-sentence definition for each term.

## 8) Parties and the electoral system
The first vote, second vote and direct mandate sound similar but do not mean the same thing.

Tip: explain the electoral system out loud in 60 seconds. If you can, you have got it.

## 9) Rights and duties in everyday life
Some questions seem easy because they come from everyday life. Even so, you need to read carefully.

Watch for key words such as "must", "may", "prohibited", "by law".

## 10) Precise wording in the answer options
Often an answer is almost right. But one small word makes it wrong.

Exam rule:
- First read the whole question.
- Then read all four answers.
- Finally compare the two answers that seem "similarly right".

## How to train difficult questions effectively
1. Mark every wrong answer in your app.
2. Revise exactly these questions after 24 hours, 3 days and 7 days.
3. Note the reason for the mistake: knowledge, term, reading error.
4. Practise regularly in exam mode with a time limit.

This way you learn from every mistake.

## Quick summary
- Difficult questions often arise from similar terms.
- Understanding helps more than memorising.
- Your own mistake list helps a lot.

## Frequently asked questions (FAQ)
### Are the difficult questions always the same in the real test?
The questions come from the official question catalogue. The selection varies, but difficult topics often come up again when practising.

### How many difficult questions can I get wrong?
What matters is the overall result. For naturalisation you normally need at least 17 correct answers.

### What is the best strategy shortly before the test?
Shortly before the test you should mainly revise the questions you got wrong and practise complete tests with a time limit.
