**Yes, to become a German citizen you normally need a valid passport from your country of origin.** The law requires your **identity and nationality to be established** ([Section 10(1) StAG](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/stag/__10.html)), and the passport is the main proof. **Not having a passport does not rule out naturalisation, though:** if you can show that you cannot get a passport, or that getting one cannot reasonably be expected of you, you can prove your identity in other ways.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. How your authority treats your case always depends on the individual facts.

## Why does the authority want a passport?

The naturalisation authority uses your personal details (name, date and place of birth) to run checks with security agencies and other bodies. It can only assess the other requirements reliably once it is sure you are who you say you are.

Important: **an identity check by the immigration office (Ausländerbehörde) does not bind the naturalisation authority.** Your German residence permit alone is therefore not proof of identity.

## The step model: what counts if there is no passport?

The Federal Administrative Court set out a step model in 2020 ([judgment 1 C 36.19](https://www.bverwg.de/de/230920U1C36.19.0)) and refined it in December 2025 ([judgment 1 C 27.24](https://www.bverwg.de/de/pm/2025/98)). The steps apply **one after another**, not as alternatives:

| Step | Proof |
|---|---|
| 1 | **National passport** |
| 2 | recognised passport substitute or another official photo ID, e.g. national identity card |
| 3 | other official documents, e.g. birth certificate, driving licence, school certificates |
| 4 | non-official evidence, e.g. records, information, witnesses |
| 5 | exceptionally, only your own consistent and credible statements |

**You may only move to the next step if the previous one is objectively impossible or cannot reasonably be expected of you, and you have cooperated sufficiently.** The authority may not demand a sworn affidavit.

### New since December 2025: a national ID card is no longer enough by default

In the case of a Syrian refugee, the Administrative Court of Düsseldorf had accepted his Syrian identity card. The Federal Administrative Court overturned this: **first it must be checked whether a passport is possible and reasonable.** Only then does the identity card count. If all you have is an ID card from your home country, expect the authority to ask for a passport.

## What about recognised refugees?

Recognised refugees must also establish their identity. According to the court, refugees' difficulties in providing evidence are addressed by **easing the burden of proof**, but **not by dropping the identity check altogether**.

- **Refugee travel document ("blue passport"):** it does not automatically prove your identity. If it notes that the details are based only on your own statements, it carries little weight.
- **Fear of losing protection status:** according to the Federal Administrative Court, applying for a passport at your embassy for naturalisation purposes generally does **not** mean you voluntarily place yourself under your home country's protection. As a rule, it does not lead to your refugee status being revoked.
- **Danger to you or your family:** going to the embassy may be unreasonable, for example after proven persecution or trauma, or if relatives back home would be at risk. You must explain this danger **concretely**; a general claim is not enough. Authorities often check first whether a representative or relatives can obtain the documents for you.
- **Subsidiary protection** alone does not usually make obtaining a passport unreasonable, according to the Berlin administrative guidance.

## My passport has expired. Is that enough?

Sometimes. An **expired passport** can still prove your identity in justified cases, if it clearly identifies you and there is no doubt about its authenticity or content. Many authorities still ask for a valid passport, so apply for a renewal early and submit the old passport as well.

## No passport: what to do

1. **Try to obtain a passport and document every step:** appointment requests, emails, refusals, fee receipts, replies from the embassy.
2. **If it is not possible, put the reasons in writing:** why is a passport impossible (e.g. the embassy issues none) or unreasonable (a concrete danger)? Your BAMF decision can help.
3. **Submit every document from the lower steps:** ID card, birth certificate, civil register extract, driving licence, school and university certificates, marriage certificate. Foreign documents usually need a certified translation.
4. **Keep your details consistent:** name, date and place of birth must match across all documents and your earlier statements. Contradictions are the quickest way to raise doubts.
5. **Meet deadlines:** if the authority has doubts, it should tell you why and give you a reasonable deadline to submit more evidence.
6. **Get help:** migration counselling services, refugee councils or lawyers specialising in migration law know how your authority works.

If reasonable doubts about your identity remain in the end, they count against you and the application can be refused.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to give up my current citizenship if I apply for a passport?

No. Since June 2024 you no longer have to give up your previous citizenship when you naturalise. The passport only serves as proof of identity.

### Can I apply while I am waiting for my passport?

With many authorities, yes. Say openly that you have applied for a passport and submit proof of that application. You will only be naturalised once your identity is established.

### Is my German residence permit or travel document for foreigners enough?

Not automatically. Both are German documents. They can support the identity check but do not replace it, especially if they note that your details are based on your own statements.
