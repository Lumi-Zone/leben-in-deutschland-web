The citizenship test is not impossible. That is exactly what makes it tricky: many candidates study hard, feel confident and then lose points to small traps. Not because they know nothing, but because they read the question too quickly or mix up two terms.

The good news: you can train yourself out of these mistakes. Once you know the typical patterns, you can improve your score quickly.

## The facts in brief: how the test is structured
The official question catalogue consists of **300 general questions** and **10 questions about your federal state**. In the exam you answer **33 multiple-choice questions**. For naturalisation you normally need at least **17 correct answers**.

To help you prepare, the BAMF offers an **[Online Test Centre](https://www.bamf.de/DE/Themen/Integration/ZugewanderteTeilnehmende/OnlineTestcenter/online-testcenter-node.html)**. There you can work through the question catalogue interactively and practise sample papers.

## Trap 1: You only learn the correct answer
Many people click through the questions and only remember the correct option. That works until two similar answers pop up in your head.

Better: after each question, also read the wrong answers. Ask yourself: why are they wrong? This is exactly what helps you see more quickly in the real test which answer can be ruled out.

## Trap 2: The wrong federal state
The state questions seem small, but they count. If you live in Bavaria and accidentally practise with questions for Berlin, you are training for the wrong test.

Set your federal state right at the start. Do not leave the state questions until the last evening; revise them regularly throughout your preparation.

## Trap 3: "Bundestag", "Bundesrat" and "Bundesregierung" blur together
These three terms are classics. They all sound official, but they have different tasks.

Remember the simple distinction:

- Bundestag: directly elected and passes laws
- Bundesrat: represents the federal states
- Bundesregierung (federal government): carries out government policy

If you always learn these three institutions together, the risk of mixing them up drops considerably.

## Trap 4: Small words decide the answer
Words such as "not", "always", "only", "may" or "must" change the entire meaning. If you read the question too quickly, you overlook exactly these signal words.

Train a fixed routine: first read the whole question, then mark the signal word, then compare the answers. This takes a few seconds and saves many points.

## Trap 5: You practise without a sense of time
60 minutes sounds like a lot. Even so, it is important to take several complete tests in one go. Practising single questions feels easier than 33 questions in a row.

Do at least three exam simulations:

1. without breaks
2. with a time limit
3. with an analysis of your mistakes afterwards

This shows you whether your knowledge stays stable under exam conditions.

## Trap 6: Active and passive voting rights
These terms are often confused. Active means you vote yourself. Passive means you can be elected.

A simple memory aid helps: **Active = I do something. Passive = I can be elected to a position.**

## Trap 7: Learning history only as dates
History questions stick better if you put events in order. Individual dates without context are harder to remember.

Build yourself a mini timeline with a few fixed points: National Socialism, founding of the Federal Republic, the GDR, building of the Wall, reunification. You will then find it much easier to place detailed questions.

## Trap 8: You revise your mistakes too late
The most valuable questions are not the ones you answer correctly straight away. The most valuable questions are your mistakes.

Revise wrong questions in this rhythm:

- on the same day
- after 24 hours
- after 3 days
- after 7 days

This moves the answer from short-term memory into a more stable memory.

## Trap 9: You go into the exam happy with 17 correct answers
17 correct answers are enough as the threshold. As a study goal, however, that is tight. A small lapse in concentration can then hurt.

Set your personal goal higher. If you regularly answer 25 or more questions correctly in simulations, you will go into exam day much more relaxed.

## The quick 20-minute training plan
If you have little time, do not study "just anything". Use short, clear blocks:

- 5 minutes: revise questions you got wrong
- 5 minutes: practise the federal state questions
- 5 minutes: study one topic in a targeted way, such as the Bundestag or fundamental rights
- 5 minutes: a mini test without interruption

This rhythm is better than one long, chaotic session per week.

## Quick summary
- The most common traps in the citizenship test come from mix-ups, reading too quickly and not revising mistakes.
- Learn not only the correct answers but also the wrong options.
- Practise the federal state questions, institutions and signal words regularly.
- 17 correct answers are the threshold, but a higher practice goal gives you confidence.

## Frequently asked questions (FAQ)
### Which trap costs the most points in the citizenship test?
Very often it is reading mistakes and confused institutions. "Bundestag", "Bundesrat" and "Bundesregierung" in particular should be second nature.

### Do I really have to practise all 310 questions?
Yes, it makes sense: the 300 general questions and the 10 questions for your federal state form the basis of your preparation.

### How many exam simulations should I do?
At least three complete simulations are recommended. Even better is to keep practising until you are consistently well above 17 correct answers.

### What do I do if I keep getting the same questions wrong?
Note the reason for the mistake: term confused, question read too quickly or knowledge missing. Then revise exactly these questions at fixed intervals.

### Is studying on my phone enough?
For many people it is enough for preparation. What matters, though, is that you also simulate complete tests without distraction and do not just click through single questions on the side.
