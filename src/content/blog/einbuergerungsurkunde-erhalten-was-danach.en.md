When you **receive your naturalisation certificate** (Einbürgerungsurkunde), you become a German citizen the moment it is handed over. After that, you should check the details on the certificate, keep the original safe and promptly apply for a German ID card at the citizens' office (Bürgeramt) and, if needed, a passport. Also clarify what should happen to your previous residence permit and foreign passport.

The certificate is not a travel document and not a substitute for an ID card. It is the official proof of how and when you acquired German citizenship. A clear order helps with the next steps.

> **Note:** This article provides general information, not personal legal advice. Requirements for documents and appointments can differ by citizens' office, federal state and personal situation. The responsible authority gives binding information.

## Key points at a glance

- Under [Section 16 of the Nationality Act](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/stag/__16.html), naturalisation only takes effect when the naturalisation certificate is handed over.
- Check your name, date of birth, place of birth and other details, ideally during the appointment itself.
- Keep the naturalisation certificate safe permanently. It is neither an ID card nor a travel document.
- Apply for your first German ID card and, if needed, passport in person at the responsible citizens' office.
- As a rule, bring the naturalisation certificate, your existing identity documents and any civil status certificates requested. Your citizens' office sets the exact list.
- Do not destroy residence permits or previous travel documents yourself. Ask the issuing or responsible office how to deal with them.
- Before travelling abroad, check which valid documents you need for leaving, entering and returning.

## From when are you really German?

A positive decision, a payment request or an invitation to the ceremony is not yet enough. What is legally decisive is the **handing over of the naturalisation certificate**. The federal government's official information portal also explains: with the certificate you are a German citizen and can apply for German identity documents.

Before it is handed over, you make the solemn declaration required by law. Some authorities hand over the certificate at an individual appointment, others at a naturalisation ceremony. What counts is the actual handover.

If your family was naturalised together, check that there is a separate certificate for each naturalised person. The naturalisation of one parent does not automatically mean that all children were naturalised as well. You will find helpful tips on planning together in the article **[Naturalisation with children](/en/blog/einbuergerung-mit-kindern/)**.

## Step 1: Check the details on the certificate immediately

Read the certificate calmly before you leave the appointment. In particular, compare:

- first name and surname, including all spellings
- birth name, if it is listed
- date and place of birth
- the date of handover
- details of children, if they were naturalised with you

If you discover a possible error, raise it directly with the nationality authority. Do not change or add anything to the certificate yourself. If your name is spelled differently in your foreign passport, birth certificate and German registers, further clarification may be needed.

Photograph or scan the certificate for your private records, but do not let the original out of your hands unnecessarily. For the first application for German documents you often have to present it in the original. So keep it separate from the documents you carry with you every day.

## Step 2: Make an appointment at the citizens' office

With the certificate you can apply for a German ID card, a passport or both. Normally the ID card or passport authority at your main place of residence is responsible, usually the citizens' office. Applications must be made in person because the authority checks your identity and records biometric data.

German citizens aged 16 and over who are subject to the general registration obligation are, in principle, required to have an identity document. Under [Section 1 of the ID Card Act](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/pauswg/__1.html), this can also be met with a valid German passport. In practice, the ID card is usually the obvious choice for everyday life in Germany; a passport is mainly needed for travel to countries where the ID card is not enough.

Available appointments, fees and processing times differ locally. So check the current service page of your citizens' office and book early. If you need an identity document very soon, ask whether a provisional document is possible in your case. A holiday booking alone does not guarantee faster issue.

## Step 3: Prepare the documents for the ID card or passport

The [federal portal on the ID card](https://verwaltung.bund.de/leistungsverzeichnis/DE/leistung/99008001012000) and the [federal portal on the passport](https://verwaltung.bund.de/leistungsverzeichnis/DE/leistung/99085001012000) refer you to the responsible local authority. Which proof is required in an individual case also depends on whether your names and civil status are already clearly recorded in the population register.

Typically you should have ready:

- the original naturalisation certificate
- your previous foreign passport or ID card
- if applicable, birth certificate, marriage certificate or proof of the name you use
- for children, additional documents on custody and consent
- a current biometric photo via an approved digital process
- means of payment as specified by the citizens' office

Since May 2025, photos for new official documents are generally transmitted to the authority digitally and via a secure channel. Depending on the citizens' office, the photo is taken on site or transmitted by a registered photo service. Check before your appointment which option your authority offers; a printed passport photo is generally no longer enough.

Do not have expensive translations or new certificates made as a precaution. First ask which document, which copy and which translation is accepted. This is especially important if names were transliterated differently in Latin and non-Latin script.

## ID card, passport or both?

The **ID card** (Personalausweis) mainly serves as a compact proof of identity in everyday life. Its online ID function lets you use many digital administrative and contract services. It is also enough for travel to numerous European countries, although you should always check the current entry requirements of your destination.

The **passport** is the classic document for travel outside Europe. Whether you need it straight away depends on your travel plans. If no such trip is planned for now, you can apply for the ID card first and add the passport later.

The **naturalisation certificate** has a different purpose: it proves the acquisition of citizenship. Do not take it with you on trips as a substitute for a passport or ID card. Keep the original protected and only use copies where a copy is enough.

## Step 4: Clarify your residence permit and previous passport

Once naturalisation takes effect, you no longer rely on a right of residence as a foreign national. This does not mean, however, that you should cut up or throw away your electronic residence permit card yourself. Ask at the handover or at the responsible immigration office whether and how the card should be returned. If anything is unclear, have the intended procedure explained to you.

The status of your previous national passport also cannot be answered across the board under German law. Germany generally accepts multiple citizenship. The law of your previous country of citizenship may, however, attach other consequences to naturalisation. Ask its embassy or consulate before you continue to use or hand in the passport. Our overview of **[dual citizenship](/en/blog/doppelte-staatsbuergerschaft/)** explains this distinction in more detail.

Important: neither the nationality authority nor the citizens' office can decide with binding effect for another country whether its citizenship or passport continues.

## Step 5: Inform important offices in a targeted way

After naturalisation you do not have to write to every company indiscriminately. Work according to actual need. A notification is particularly useful if an office has stored your nationality or ID details, or if your name changed at the same time.

Check, for example:

1. **Employer:** an update can make sense for personnel records or proof of employment status. Your tax identification number and social insurance number do not change automatically through naturalisation.
2. **Banks and insurers:** ask whether new identity data are needed once your German ID card has been issued.
3. **Family benefits office, benefit agencies or student finance:** report the change if your nationality is relevant to an ongoing case or the office asks you to.
4. **School, university or other authorities:** update details where there is an active record and the information matters.

Do not send the naturalisation certificate unsolicited as an open email attachment to many recipients. Ask for a secure transmission channel and whether the new ID card is enough instead.

## Travelling right after naturalisation: plan especially carefully

There may be a transition period between the handover of the certificate and receiving German travel documents. The certificate itself does not entitle you to cross borders. At the same time, whether you can still use your previous passport may depend on whether your former citizenship continues.

So do not plan a trip on the assumption that the naturalisation certificate will be accepted at the border. Clarify in advance with the citizens' office which German documents can be issued in time and, if necessary, with the embassy of your previous country of citizenship what status the old passport has. Also check the current entry requirements of your destination.

If, on the other hand, your naturalisation procedure is still ongoing and you have not yet received the certificate, other questions apply. The article **[Holidays during the naturalisation process](/en/blog/urlaub-waehrend-einbuergerung/)** covers this.

## Practical checklist after the handover

- [ ] details on every naturalisation certificate checked
- [ ] original stored safely and a private backup copy made
- [ ] service page of the responsible citizens' office checked
- [ ] appointment booked for the ID card and, if needed, passport
- [ ] naturalisation certificate and existing identity documents prepared
- [ ] civil status certificates and proof of name gathered according to the authority's list
- [ ] digital photo arranged according to local requirements
- [ ] handling of the residence permit clarified with the responsible office
- [ ] status of the previous passport checked with the embassy or consulate if needed
- [ ] only genuinely relevant offices informed of the new details
- [ ] travel abroad planned only with clarified, valid travel documents

## Frequently asked questions (FAQ)

### Can I travel straight away with the naturalisation certificate?

No. The naturalisation certificate proves the acquisition of German citizenship, but it is neither a passport nor an ID card. For a trip you need a valid travel document that is recognised for that purpose.

### Do I have to apply for an ID card and a passport at the same time?

No. Depending on your needs you can apply for only an ID card, only a passport or both. But note the obligation to have an identity document: adults and young people aged 16 and over who are required to register in Germany generally need a valid German ID card or passport.

### Which documents does the citizens' office need after naturalisation?

The naturalisation certificate is almost always central. Often your previous passport or ID card, civil status certificates, proof of the name you use and a digital biometric photo are added. The current list from your citizens' office is authoritative.

### May I keep my previous foreign passport?

That depends on the law of the issuing country. Germany generally no longer requires you to give up your previous citizenship when naturalising. But another country may regulate loss of citizenship or special reporting obligations differently. Ask its embassy or consulate.

### What do I do if my name is misspelled on the certificate?

Do not change the certificate yourself. Report the possible error to the nationality authority immediately and present the documents that show the correct spelling. Ideally, resolve the discrepancy before applying for German identity documents.

### Do my tax ID or social insurance number change?

Naturalisation alone does not normally give you a new tax identification number or a new social insurance number. If your name changes, however, you should check whether the stored personal data need to be updated.

## Next step

Now open the website of your citizens' office and search for "Personalausweis nach Einbürgerung" (ID card after naturalisation) or "erster deutscher Reisepass" (first German passport). Note the documents required there and book the appointment. Then put the naturalisation certificate, your previous passport and civil status documents in one folder. This way you avoid a missing document delaying your first German ID card.
